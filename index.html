The Architects of Democracy
507 BCE · Athens
scroll

In 507 BCE, Athens captured a unique spot in the hearts of historians. The radical revolution that swept over this Greek city-state birthed the favorite child of Western political thought. An aristocratic regime fell to the voices of men congregated on the sunny top of Pnyx Hill under Cleisthenes. It fell to democracy.

It did not.

Democracy may find its linguistic roots in Greek, but not its actual origin. In fact, the Greek roots themselves reveal the great paradox of Athenian democracy: dēmos, or the people, constitutes kratos, or power.

But it was not quite the people who captured Athens at Pnyx Hill. After all, half of the Athenian dēmos stayed sequestered within this system, becoming mere property of their tyrants.

No, Athens was never a democracy; it simply stretched aristocracy to form a patriarchy.

And while Athens may wrongly claim the honor of being the origin of democracy, it can hardly claim sole ownership when it comes to patriarchy. Throughout time and space, civilizations artificially bifurcated their populations into two groups:

male citizens and female property.

From the roads of Rome to the bazaars of Persia to the watchtowers of China, patriarchy reigned supreme, suppressing true democracy.

The colonization of the Americas saw horrors on a scale that stands out like an ugly welt even in humanity's violent historical tapestry.

Fifty-five million people, 90% of the Indigenous population, fell into their own earth under the onslaught of disease, guns, slavery, and starvation. And along with those people fell the last bastions of gender-parallel societies and participatory democracies such as the Haudenosaunee Confederacy.

From the ashes of that egalitarianism rose the modern icon of both democracy and patriarchy. Aptly founded in Independence Hall in Philadelphia, the City of Brotherly Love, its founders defiantly declared, “All men are created equal,” conveniently ignoring the sisters of their nation.

The inherently undemocratic nature of that foundation led the United States to build a system that was little more than Athenian patriarchal tyranny masquerading in the robes of performative Enlightenment thought.

The Enlightenment itself was built on the writings of misogynists like Voltaire, Rousseau, and Locke; therefore, it cannot be claimed that the movement was democratic or egalitarian in any true sense.

Patriarchy's foundational claim of sex-based superiority directly contradicts democracy's foundational claim of universal human dignity. Thus, it is only natural that the ideological descendants of these men, such as Jefferson and Adams, founded an undemocratic and patriarchal nation.

Neither the American Eastern Seaboard nor the Athenian hills birthed democracy. Making the presence of a Y chromosome a prerequisite for political involvement is not of the people, by the people, and for the people; it is of the men, by the men, and for the men.

To find the first Western democratic thinkers, we must shift our gaze to Christine de Pizan, Abigail Adams, Mary Wollstonecraft, and Olympe de Gouges.

And for the founding mothers of American democracy, we look to Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Susan B. Anthony, Alice Paul, and Carrie Chapman Catt.

Electoral Act 1893 · New Zealand

By signing the Electoral Act into law on September 19, 1893, Governor Lord Glasgow made New Zealand the very first electoral democracy in the world. Lambton Quay proved to be the initial spark of democracy, with Kate Sheppard striking the match.

Despite these prolific leaders, the United States found itself on the tail end of this movement. Even with Edith Wilson serving as the de facto chief executive in the Oval Office, American women were forced to wait years before gaining the right to vote.

August 18, 1920, marked the Independence Day for half of Americans and the true birthday of American democracy.

Sadly, that day was far from the death of patriarchy. Despite being bravely fought by waves of feminists, issues such as the gender pay gap, casual sexism, and violence against women remain specters of Philadelphia and Athens.

By reinforcing the narrative that women's rights are simply an addition to democracy, we amplify the idea that women are not part of “We the People.”
